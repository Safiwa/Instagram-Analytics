# Median Beats Mean Only for Typical Posts

No — Modash's median-likes-over-roughly-three-months formula is not more valid and accurate in any universal sense; it is more robust for one question and less responsive for another. For *"what will a typical post from this creator earn?"* the median is statistically better motivated, because Instagram engagement is extremely heavy-tailed and the mean has a breakdown point of 1/n while the median's is about 50% ([NIST](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm); [Rousseeuw 2011](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/widm.2)). For *"what did this account's recent content actually produce?"* the 12-post mean of likes plus comments is the more direct estimator and reacts faster. Adding comments to the numerator slightly improves coverage of publicly visible interaction but changes the number by only ~1–2% in most accounts; aggregation and window choice move results far more ([LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide)). Crucially, no head-to-head study validates either recipe against a true engagement propensity or campaign outcomes, and both inherit the same follower-denominator weaknesses ([Captiv8](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/4274052/%23NoFilter-How%20to%20Uncover%20the%20Fakes_Captiv8%20Report_Final.pdf); [HypeAuditor](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it)). The honest verdict is that "accuracy" here resolves to *typical performance versus recent output*, not better method versus worse method.

## The two formulas answer different questions

The comparison is between two legitimate but non-equivalent conventions, not a corrected metric versus a flawed one. Modash's current Instagram engagement rate is the **median number of likes per post divided by total followers** (× 100), with the help center stating *"All calculations are based on roughly the last three months of content"* and explaining that *"one viral post shouldn't trick you into thinking a creator consistently gets high engagement"* ([Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it)). The competing method — average likes plus average comments divided by followers over up to 12 recent posts — is itself a common public-data convention, used at scale by Virev across 431,494 accounts and documented by OwlStat across 22,646 accounts ([Virev](https://virev.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks); [OwlStat](https://owlstat.io/instagram-engagement-benchmarks)). There is no industry standard that would arbitrate between them: providers differ simultaneously on numerator (likes, likes+comments, or likes+comments+saves+shares), denominator (followers, reach, or views), aggregation (mean, median, pooled ratio), and window (12 posts, 30 posts, 2 months, 90 days) ([Buffer research](https://buffer.com/resources/state-of-social-media-engagement-2026); [Sprout Social](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/instagram-engagement-rate)). Even Modash's own documentation uses three different ER constructions — a median-based Discovery rate, per-creator averages in Campaign analytics, and views/impressions-based rates — and explicitly says *"the same creator may show different ER% values in each context … Both are valid, they just measure slightly different things"* ([Modash campaign help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/13717084-understanding-campaign-analytics)).

Two caveats undermine any claim that Modash's version is the more precisely specified benchmark. First, Modash's own live pages conflict on the window: the authoritative help article says **roughly three months**, while the public calculator, blog, and API cheat sheet still say **the last two months**, the last of which adds *"up to around 500 posts"* ([Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator); [Modash blog](https://www.modash.io/blog/influencer-engagement-rate); [API cheat sheet](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/14035619-api-cheat-sheet-limits-filters-metrics)). No primary source gives an exact calendar-day rule, a minimum post count, or says whether the follower denominator is a current snapshot, a per-post value, or a window average. Second, the numerators measure different constructs: Instagram's own definition treats interactions as *"likes, comments, saves and shares,"* and academic work finds likes and comments correlate only moderately (Spearman ρ = 0.58), with comments rarer and more effortful ([Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/788388387972460); [arXiv 2301.06815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06815)). The 12-post method therefore has better *content validity* as a measure of publicly visible interaction, while Modash's likes-only rate is a deliberately simplified proxy for typical light-touch response — a difference of construct, not just accuracy.

## Viral spikes favor the median but precision favors the mean

The statistical case for Modash's median is real and rests on the shape of engagement data. In a 245,559-post corpus the **median was 89 likes versus a mean of 4,222 — a 47-fold gap** — with the top 1% of posts holding 64.1% of all likes, comments, and shares, and the mean ER (3.52%) exceeding the median (2.41%) ([buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks)). Independent academic work reaches the same distributional conclusion: log-log CCDFs of Instagram likes and comments are nearly straight lines, and a power-law fit reports an exponent of γ = 1.391 for likes ([WebSci 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3614419.3644029); [arXiv 1406.7751](https://arxiv.org/html/1406.7751v1)). Against that backdrop the median's **50% breakdown point and bounded influence** are decisive: a single viral or bot-driven post cannot move it without limit, whereas a single arbitrarily large value can move the mean without limit ([Rousseeuw 2011](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/widm.2)). A 12-post illustration for @nasa found mean likes of 511,691 versus a median of 300,002, with five ways of computing a rate from the same pull ranging from 0.184% to 0.574% of followers ([monid.ai](https://monid.ai/blog/guides/are-instagram-engagement-trackers-accurate)).

The median is not free, and at the sample sizes at issue neither estimator dominates. Under clean normal data the sample median's asymptotic relative efficiency versus the mean is only **2/π ≈ 64%**, and with 12 observations the median is simply the average of the 6th and 7th sorted values — it discards the magnitudes of the extremes ([MATH3714 notes](https://seehuhn.github.io/MATH3714/S19-efficiency.html); [NIST](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm)). Research on skewed distributions finds the sample median is mean-biased upward at small n (about 15 ms at n = 10 in one ex-Gaussian simulation), while the sample mean is mean-unbiased but median-biased under skew — with the conclusion that *"no method dominates"* ([Rousselet & Wilcox 2020](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/1630)). A **10–20% trimmed mean** is a documented compromise that keeps most of the mean's efficiency near normality while muting outliers ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4205532)). So the mean is neither naive nor invalid: it is the nearer-to-unbiased estimate of *average engagement per post including the viral upside*, which is the right target if the goal is total engagement delivered across a campaign's post set. The word "valid" is estimand-dependent, and the heavy tails that make the median attractive for forecasting a normal post are precisely the tails that make the mean attractive for estimating total output.

## Three months buys stability while twelve posts buy recency

The window choice introduces a second, independent tradeoff that the mean-versus-median debate obscures. **Neither window is cadence-neutral.** A fixed ~3-month window equalizes calendar exposure but leaves the sample size uncontrolled: at one post per week it contains roughly 13 posts, at four per week roughly 52, and a 500-post cap can bind for the busiest accounts ([Vista Social](https://vistasocial.com/research/social-media-benchmarks); [API cheat sheet](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/14035619-api-cheat-sheet-limits-filters-metrics)). A fixed 12-post window equalizes n and therefore the influence of any single outlier, but yields a variable calendar span — about two weeks for a daily poster and up to a year for a monthly one ([Virev](https://virev.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks)). The practical consequence is asymmetric: Modash's rate is noisier for sparse accounts and its two-to-four-week refresh cycle adds lag, while the 12-post rate describes a quarter of history for a slow account and only days for a fast one ([Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it)).

Recency is a genuine advantage for the 12-post method, not merely a defect. Instagram interactions are front-loaded and short-lived — the median time for a post to accumulate 95% of its interactions is **33 hours**, with roughly half arriving in the first two hours — so a rolling 12-post average reflects a creator's current format and quality faster than a three-month pool ([Springer, SN Applied Network Science 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13278-022-00928-2)). Freshness is not free: one large corpus found its newest posts at a 1.91% median, plausibly because they were still accumulating engagement, and pinned posts can distort a small sample — in one audit, dropping three pinned posts moved the mean from 0.490% to 0.437% and the median from 0.287% to 0.248% of followers ([buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks); [monid.ai](https://monid.ai/blog/guides/are-instagram-engagement-trackers-accurate)). Sparse accounts are a shared weakness: Modash publishes no minimum post count and only a qualitative *"haven't posted recently enough"* rule, while practitioner guidance ranges from *"under ten, treat it as an indication"* to *"draw no conclusion from fewer than about 40 posts"* ([Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it); [ProveitGo](https://www.proveitgo.com/blog/engagement-rate-different-every-tool); [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks)).

| Definition of "accuracy" | Median likes ÷ followers, ~3 months | Mean likes+comments ÷ followers, ≤12 posts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical/next-post performance | Stronger — outlier-resistant by design | Weaker — one hit lifts the mean |
| Stability over time | Stronger, but refresh lag and sparse-account risk | Weaker to moderate; fast-moving |
| Comparability across profiles | Equal n uncontrolled; fixed calendar span | Equal n; variable calendar span |
| Responsiveness to recent change | Slower (2–4 week refresh, 3-month pool) | Faster (rolling 12 posts) |
| Breadth of interaction captured | Narrower (likes only) | Slightly broader (comments ~1–2% of public interactions) |
| Link to business outcomes | Unproven | Unproven |

## Follower counts and fake audiences weaken both methods equally

The most consequential validity problem is shared and sits upstream of all the aggregation choices. **Follower count is a changing, partially fake, and imperfect proxy for who actually saw a post**, and neither the median nor the mean repairs it. Average Instagram follower growth is around 7.5% per month, so a 90-day window can see **10–25% denominator growth**; applying a single current follower count to every post in that window systematically understates earlier posts' rates ([Buffer benchmarks](https://buffer.com/social-media-benchmarks/instagram); [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/788388387972460)). A Captiv8 analysis of 5,000 randomly selected influencer accounts per category found that **more than 11% of engagement on sponsored Instagram posts came from fraudulent accounts**, while bot-like engagement pods manufacture short, similar comments that specifically inflate a likes+comments numerator ([Captiv8](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/4274052/%23NoFilter-How%20to%20Uncover%20the%20Fakes_Captiv8%20Report_Final.pdf); [Kim & Han 2020](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-60975-7_10)). Fake followers push both rates down, bought comments push the 12-post rate up, and the net bias for any given account is unknown.

Follower-based rates are also not scale-invariant: Modash's own empirical medians fall from 3.51% at 1–5k followers to 0.38–0.57% at 10k–100k, and academic work finds an inverted-U relationship between follower count and engagement ([Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator); [Wies, Bleier & Edeling, JMR 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00222429221125131)). Tier-stratified comparison is mandatory under either estimator. Views- or reach-based rates would answer the content-effectiveness question more cleanly — HypeAuditor moved to likes+comments ÷ views in 2026 because *"follower count no longer predicts how many people see a post,"* and Hootsuite calls reach-based ER the most accurate measure of content quality — but reach and saves are unavailable to third parties observing someone else's feed, and Instagram itself labels some reach figures as estimated ([HypeAuditor help](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it); [Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/average-engagement-rate)). For public-account screening, the denominator flaw is a reason to treat both numbers as screens, not verdicts — Modash agrees that ER *"isn't proof of authenticity or purchasing intent"* ([Modash blog](https://www.modash.io/blog/influencer-engagement-rate)).

## Cross-formula benchmark comparisons can reverse the verdict

The practical accuracy risk is not which estimator a product computes but what users do with the number afterward. **A rate from one formula cannot be placed on another tool's benchmark table as a percentile.** On a single invented post, likes÷followers gives 5.0%, likes+comments÷followers 5.25%, likes+comments÷views 4.2%, and all four interactions÷reach 7.05% — four legitimate engagement rates differing by nearly three percentage points ([OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks)). Aggregation alone changes the answer: for three posts with rates of 1%, 20%, and 15%, the median of post rates is 15%, the ratio of separate medians is 10%, and the pooled rate is about 4.62% — *"a median of ratios is not generally a ratio of medians"* ([SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools)). Cross-formula comparisons can even reverse direction rather than merely shift scale: the same Rival IQ TikTok dataset shows per-follower ER falling as accounts grow while per-view ER rises ([OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks)).

Modash's own guidance illustrates the trap. Its help page calls **3–7% "good" for 10k–100k followers**, yet its calculator's empirical medians across **84M+ Instagram profiles** are 0.57% for 10k–50k and 0.38% for 50k–100k; the prescriptive ranges sit above the company's own observed database medians ([Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it); [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator); [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks), a commercial competitor whose critique should be read with that context). Published benchmark spread is largely denominator- and population-driven — brand-account tables from Socialinsider and Rival IQ describe brands, not creators — and within a single follower tier the interquartile range can be wider than the gap between tiers ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram); [FlowFame](https://flowfame.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks)). A directional screening statement within the same tier and niche is defensible, but small gaps should be treated as ties, and terms like "top 25%" borrowed from another formula's table are not. The strongest cross-provider consensus is not about which estimator wins but about labeling: *"a properly labeled 0.6% tells you more than an unexplained 4% ever will"* ([Followerus](https://followerus.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate)).

## Conclusion

Switching a fixed-formula creator ranker from a 12-post mean to Modash's median/~3-month recipe would probably improve stability and typical-post predictiveness, but would degrade responsiveness, break comparability with the product's own historical series and with 12-post peer benchmarks, and — most importantly — would not be an "accuracy upgrade" on any validated definition. No study found tests rank correlation, top-k overlap, or business outcomes for the two estimators on the same creators, and even the peer-reviewed literature treats ranking ground truth as estimator-dependent: one influencer dataset reports a mean ER of 0.038 against a median of 0.029, and influencers selected purely on 7–12% ER showed only average audience retention and sometimes high fraud scores ([InfluencerRank, ICWSM 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/22162/21941/26225); [ALCAM/ICWSM 2021](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/18060/17863/21555)). The decision should therefore be framed as a change in estimand, not correctness.

The deeper implication is that the accuracy debate is mis-specified. The largest error sources — a growing and partly fake follower denominator, format mix, hidden likes, and benchmark tables whose formulas and populations do not match the number being judged — are common to both methods and dwarf the ~1–2% contribution of comments or the mean–median gap for well-behaved accounts. A product that adopts a **median of up to 30 posts from the last 90 days with a minimum post count and explicit exclusions**, publishes its formula and recomputes its own tier benchmarks, and displays a volatility or outlier flag, would gain most of the robustness rationale without inheriting Modash's undocumented "~3 months" ambiguity ([Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator)). Until someone runs the head-to-head validation study, honesty about what the number measures is a bigger accuracy gain than switching the estimator.
