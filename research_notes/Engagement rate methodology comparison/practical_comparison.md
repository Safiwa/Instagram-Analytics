# Practical accuracy of the two engagement-rate methods for a public Instagram analytics product

Scope note for the report writer: the "this site" method is described in the assignment as `(average likes + average comments) ÷ followers` over up to 12 recent posts. The closest published description found is OWM's own documentation: "For a creator who has connected Instagram, the profile works the rate out from the account's average likes plus average comments divided by followers, a mean and not a median." [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks). I did not inspect the product code (research-only assignment), so treat that as a documented description, not an independent verification.

## Key Question 1: What do major analytics tools and industry guides actually use (mean ER, median ER, window length, likes-only vs likes+comments, reach-based ER)?

### Takeaway
There is no industry-standard engagement rate: providers differ simultaneously on numerator (likes only, likes+comments, likes+comments+saves+shares), denominator (followers, reach/impressions, views), aggregation (mean of post rates, median of post rates, median counts, pooled ratios), and window (12 posts, 30 posts, 2 months, 90 days, 3 months). Both methods in question sit inside this legitimate spread; neither is an outlier.

### Cited Findings
- **Modash (Discovery/ER shown on creator profiles):** Instagram ER = **median likes ÷ followers**, explicitly "All calculations are based on roughly the last three months of content." Stated rationale: "one viral post shouldn't trick you into thinking a creator consistently gets high engagement. The median filters out those spikes and shows you what *typical* performance actually looks like." TikTok = median likes+comments+shares+saves ÷ views; YouTube = median likes+comments ÷ views. ER recalculated every 2–4 weeks; pinned posts are included if inside the window; hidden likes make an accurate ER impossible. [Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it)
- **Modash public calculator (conflicting window):** same formula (`median likes per post ÷ total followers × 100`) but says "Modash uses the **last two months** of content, and the median rather than the mean, so one viral post can't drag the whole figure up." Benchmark of medians across **84M+ Instagram profiles**: 3.51% (1k–5k), 1.00% (5k–10k), 0.57% (10k–50k), 0.38% (50k–100k), 0.41% (100k–500k), 0.45% (500k–1M), 0.51% (>1M); quality bands (e.g. 10k–50k: high >1.27%, average 0.49–0.65%, low <0.24%). [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator)
- The 2-month vs ~3-month discrepancy between Modash's own help page and calculator is explicitly flagged by an independent methodology comparison; the same source notes Modash's Discovery uses median while its Campaign dashboard uses per-creator averages, and that the two "are valid, they just measure slightly different things." [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools); [Modash campaign help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/13717084-understanding-campaign-analytics)
- Modash's own blog: "Instagram: median likes ÷ followers", "TikTok: average likes ÷ average views", "YouTube: average likes ÷ average views"; "One viral post can drag an average wildly out of proportion ... the median gives you a steadier, more honest look"; "Different tools use different formulas and timeframes, so if there's a mismatch, it usually comes down to methodology"; rates are not directly comparable across platforms. [Modash blog](https://www.modash.io/blog/influencer-engagement-rate)
- **HypeAuditor:** current Instagram ER = **likes + comments ÷ views**; shares and saves are excluded because Instagram treats them as private data only fully visible to the account owner; if there are too few recent Reels it falls back to a follower-based calculation and labels it; "At HypeAuditor, we use the latest **30 publications** to calculate an influencer's ER," with individual content rates summarized by a **median**. [HypeAuditor help](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it); [HypeAuditor August 2026 methodology summary](https://hypeauditor.com/digest-august-26/)
- HypeAuditor reportedly moved to views in 2026 "because follower count no longer predicts how many people see a post"; its US medians on views are nearly flat by tier (4.33% nano → 3.72% mega), while its follower-based 2025-report figures were much lower. [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks)
- **Socialinsider:** average ER per post by followers = `(likes + comments) ÷ followers × 100`, then **averaged** across all posts; 67.5M posts from 579,000 brand accounts; published 0.48% for 2025 and 0.45% in Q3 2026 by followers. Its own methodology note contains a dating inconsistency ("January 2024 – December 2026"; "This study contains 2025 values, presented as 2026"). [Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram)
- **Rival IQ / Quid:** median of total interactions ÷ follower count; 150 randomly selected brand companies per industry across 18 industries; Instagram all-industry median 0.30% in 2026, down from 0.36% the previous edition. [IImagined benchmark compilation](https://iimagined.ai/stats/instagram-engagement-benchmarks-2026); [Alpine Reach](https://www.alpinereach.io/blog/good-engagement-rate-on-instagram)
- **Buffer:** ER = engagements ÷ reach (closest available denominator: impressions or views where reach is unavailable); describes its medians as "typical performance in Buffer's dataset, rather than universal benchmarks. (We wish universal benchmarks existed. They don't.)" Its Instagram benchmark page reports a median ER of 4.1% from 27M+ posts / 273,000 accounts. [Buffer research](https://buffer.com/resources/state-of-social-media-engagement-2026); [Buffer Instagram benchmarks](https://buffer.com/social-media-benchmarks/instagram)
- **Hootsuite:** reach-based ER is "the most accurate measure of content quality, because it only counts people who actually saw the post"; follower-based ER is "the most widely reported engagement rate formula, and the one used for the industry benchmarks below. Use it when you need to compare your performance to published averages." Published Instagram all-industry average ~4.2%. [Hootsuite benchmark guide](https://blog.hootsuite.com/average-engagement-rate); [Hootsuite calculation guide](https://blog.hootsuite.com/calculate-engagement-rate)
- **Sprout Social** publishes three denominators side by side — by followers ("measuring how your core audience responds"), by reach ("content effectiveness with actual viewers"), by impressions ("repeat engagement power per view") — and a broader first-party numerator that can include likes, comments, shares, saves and Story replies. [Sprout Social](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/instagram-engagement-rate)
- **12-post, likes+comments, follower-based mean is itself a common convention**, not a fringe one: Virev measured 431,494 public accounts with "average likes and average comments of the account's 12 most recent posts, divided by followers" [Virev](https://virev.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks); OwlStat used the same formula on 22,646 accounts (pinned posts left out, hidden likes excluded) [OwlStat](https://owlstat.io/instagram-engagement-benchmarks); Pinlyx reports that 97.6% of accounts in its crawl resolve to the 12 most recent posts [Pinlyx](https://pinlyx.com/research/instagram-engagement-benchmarks-2026). Practitioner guides commonly recommend "last 12" or "12 to 20 posts" [Createthat](https://www.createthat.ai/tools/instagram-engagement-rate-calculator); [Outfame](https://www.outfame.com/blog/average-instagram-engagement-rate).
- **Academic convention:** a peer-reviewed influencer-marketing study defines prior ER as the **average per-post engagement (likes + comments) over the past 90 days ÷ followers** [Wies, Bleier & Edeling, *Journal of Marketing Research*, 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00222429221125131). A separate ICWSM influencer-ranking paper reports that in its dataset the **mean ER was 0.038 and the median ER was 0.029**, i.e. the choice of statistic changes the "ground truth" for ranking [InfluencerRank, ICWSM 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/22162/21941/26225).
- Practitioner guidance on how to choose: "Rate by reach is the better measure of content quality because it ignores follower count. Rate by followers is the better measure when you want to compare against follower-based studies such as Socialinsider and Rival IQ" [Outfame](https://www.outfame.com/blog/average-instagram-engagement-rate); "Follower-based ER is therefore still useful [for public accounts]. Just call it what it is: a follower-based public engagement rate. Then compare accounts using the same formula and a similar measurement window" [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide).

### Inferences
- Both contested methods are follower-based public-data ERs; their differences are (a) mean vs median, (b) likes vs likes+comments, (c) 12 posts vs a ~2–3 month/2–4-week-refreshed window. Numerator choice is numerically the smallest of these: LikeWave found likes were a median **98.1%** of visible likes+comments across 131 accounts [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide), while OwlStat found 1.33–8.36 comments per 100 likes depending on tier [OwlStat](https://owlstat.io/instagram-engagement-benchmarks). Median-vs-mean and window choice move numbers much more.
- The strongest industry consensus is not about which estimator is best but about **labelling and like-for-like comparison**: every major source above either states its own formula or warns that cross-formula comparisons are invalid.
- "Views/reach is more accurate" is only true for the question "how did the content perform with people who saw it"; it is unavailable for other people's feed images and is not what either product under review is computing.

### Gaps
- No primary Rival IQ/Quid 2026 report PDF was fetched in this research pass; its methodology is reported here via secondary compilations that quote it directly.
- No source found that states Modash's exact number of posts per profile, minimum post count, or precise window rule for Discovery ER beyond "roughly three months" / "last two months".
- No source found that documents the 12-post site's exact exclusion rules (pinned posts, giveaways, hidden likes, still-maturing posts) beyond the general OWM description.

## Key Question 2: If two tools use different formulas, how should a user interpret a comparison against an external benchmark table? Is a directional comparison defensible, and what caveats matter most?

### Takeaway
A raw number from one formula cannot be placed on another tool's benchmark table as if it were a percentile. A coarse directional read ("roughly in line / clearly above / clearly below") is defensible only after matching numerator, denominator, population, follower tier and aggregation — and should be presented as a caveat, not a tier label. Recomputing a peer benchmark with the product's own formula is the defensible alternative.

### Cited Findings
- Common denominators produce huge spread: on one invented post, likes÷followers = 5.0%, likes+comments÷followers = 5.25%, likes+comments÷views = 4.2%, all four interactions÷reach = 7.05% — "Each is a legitimate engagement rate, and they differ by almost three percentage points on one post" [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks). Another worked example: 2.75% follower-based vs 6.75% reach-based for the same post [FlowFame](https://flowfame.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- "A creator's rate is only good or bad next to a benchmark that divides by the same thing" [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks). Four pre-comparison checks: denominator, numerator, population (brand vs creator), and mean vs median [FlowFame](https://flowfame.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- "Calling one of those numbers 'the Instagram engagement rate' without explaining the formula removes the information you need to interpret it" [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide).
- **Aggregation alone can change an answer**: for three posts with rates 1%, 20%, 15%, the median of post rates is 15%, the ratio of separate medians is 10%, the pooled rate is ~4.62%, and the unweighted mean is 12%. "A median of ratios is not generally a ratio of medians." You usually cannot convert one account-level percentage into another from the percentage alone [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools).
- **Modash's own published guidance conflicts with its own database medians**: help page calls 3–7% "good" for 10k–100k followers while its 84M-profile medians are 0.57% (10k–50k) and 0.38% (50k–100k); other help-page bands (5–10% nano, 1–3% macro) also sit above its own medians. Later's 4–8% nano / 2–4% micro table names no sample and is labelled "guidelines, not rules" [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks). This is a practitioner critique from a firm that also sells creator discovery using Modash data — note the commercial context when using it.
- **Cross-formula comparisons can reverse direction**, not just shift scale: the same Rival IQ TikTok dataset shows per-follower ER falling as accounts grow while per-view ER rises; Modash, Socialinsider and Rival IQ also disagree on the *direction* of TikTok per-view rates by tier [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- **Published benchmark spread is largely denominator-driven, not reality-driven**: 0.36%–4.42% for the same platform/year across first-page sources, and in a 245,559-post views-based corpus the median ER stays within a 0.46-point band across reach deciles from hundreds to 400M views — "reach does not tax engagement, the follower denominator does" [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- Audience population matters independently of formula: brand-account benchmarks (Socialinsider, Rival IQ/Quid) describe brands, not creators [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks); "Brands and creators behave differently, and brands engage worse" [FlowFame](https://flowfame.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- Within one tier, the peer range can be wider than the tier differences: a 5,924-account likes+comments÷followers study found 1k–10k median 2.60% but interquartile range 1.12–5.60% [Cheerful figures as reported by LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide). LikeWave's own 137-account sample had a 1.58% median like rate but a middle 50% spanning 0.17–5.86% [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide).
- Format, freshness and hidden data matter too: pinned-post handling differs (Modash includes pinned posts inside the window; many calculators tell you to exclude them), hidden likes can make public ER uncomputable, and a post still accumulating engagement will look weaker than a matured one [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools); [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).

### Inferences
- **Verdict for "showing a profile's ER against Modash's tier table":** not defensible as a tier/percentile claim when the displayed number is a 12-post mean. The two numbers differ in aggregation (mean inflated by outliers), numerator (comments add ~1–2% at most in public data), and window. The direction of the bias is predictable (a recent viral post pushes the 12-post mean up relative to a median), but the magnitude is not.
- **What is defensible:** a directional screening statement within the same follower tier and niche — e.g. "this profile's public ER is in the same broad range as / clearly above / clearly below published follower-based figures" — provided the UI states the site's own formula, window, and the benchmark's formula and population, and avoids terms like "top 25%" or "average" borrowed from Modash's table. Even then, a 12-post mean is a noisy estimator, so small gaps should be treated as ties.
- **Better product answer:** publish a peer benchmark computed with the product's own formula (e.g. a 12-post likes+comments÷followers distribution by follower tier, as Virev/OwlStat do), or show both the site's ER and a method-matched "typical post" median with their own labels. Never place one formula's number on another formula's scale.
- **Recommended disclosure language (product-owner version):** "Engagement rate shown here = average likes + comments per post ÷ followers, using up to the 12 most recent public posts. This is a follower-based *average*; it can be pulled upward by one unusually successful post and cannot see saves, shares, reach or hidden like counts. Published benchmarks use different formulas (e.g. median likes ÷ followers over ~2–3 months, or engagement ÷ views/reach). Compare this number only with benchmarks that state the same formula, window and account type; treat cross-method comparisons as directional context, not a percentile."

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that quantifies how often the two specific methods produce different tier labels or rank reversals for the same accounts.
- No published conversion factor or calibration study between median-likes÷followers and 12-post likes+comments÷followers was found.
- No reliable source found estimating how much of the Modash-vs-other-tool gap comes from aggregation vs window vs numerator for a matched set of accounts.

## Key Question 3: What does "accuracy" mean here (true propensity, stability, comparability, responsiveness), and how does each method score?

### Takeaway
The methods optimise different targets. The median/~3-month estimator is better at "what will a normal post look like" and is more outlier-stable; the 12-post mean is better at "what did this account's recent content actually produce" and reacts faster. Neither is closer to a validated "true" engagement propensity, and neither is a validated predictor of campaign outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- **Outlier robustness (favours median):** engagement counts are extremely skewed. In a 245,559-post corpus, median likes were 89 vs mean 4,222 (**47×**), median shares 5 vs mean 1,047; the top 1% of posts held 64.1% of all likes/comments/shares, and the mean ER (3.52%) exceeded the median (2.41%) [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks). A LinkedIn corpus of 100,459 posts found the mean ER **58% higher** than the median [ContentIn](https://contentin.io/blog/linkedin-engagement-benchmarks/); another benchmark dashboard reports engagement is lognormal with the mean ~**2.6×** the median [Imagine AI methodology](https://benchmark.imagineai.me/dashboard/methodology).
- **Typical-post predictive value (favours median):** with post rates 0.8%, 0.9%, 1.0%, 1.1%, 12.0%, the mean is 3.16% and the median 1.0%; "If you're deciding what performance you're likely to get from the creator's next post, 1.0% tells a much more recognizable story" [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide). Modash's stated reason for the median is the same [Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it).
- **Short-window vulnerability (favours median/longer window):** across 38 popular accounts, 21 had an average at least 2× their typical post; the set's median ER was 0.16% vs Rival IQ's 0.36% brand median [ToolzerHub](https://toolzerhub.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate). Practitioners warn "under ten [posts], treat it as an indication. Over twenty, treat it as a measurement" [ProveitGo](https://www.proveitgo.com/blog/engagement-rate-different-every-tool); another guide says "aim for 20 to 30, and be sceptical below about ten" [Xcut](https://xcut.ai/guides/how-to-find-outlier-posts); a large research corpus says "draw no conclusion from fewer than about 40 posts" [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks). OWM recommends "the last 30, or fewer for a new account" and a median [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- **Window-length tradeoff (mixed):** a 12-post window is a *fixed sample count but variable calendar span* (a daily poster's 12 posts cover ~2 weeks; a monthly poster's cover a year); Virev dealt with this by taking up to 12 posts from a 180-day window and requiring posts at least 3 days old so "likes had settled" [Virev](https://virev.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks). A ~3-month window is a *fixed span but variable sample count*; Modash refreshes every 2–4 weeks [Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it). Sparse accounts are therefore a shared weakness, not a unique one.
- **Responsiveness (favours 12-post mean):** a rolling 12-post average changes with every new post, while a 3-month median and a 2–4-week refresh cycle lag [Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it); [ProveitGo](https://www.proveitgo.com/blog/engagement-rate-different-every-tool) ("A tool reading your last 12 posts and one reading your last 25 will disagree whenever your recent posts differ from your older ones"). Freshness is not free: buzzabout notes its newest posts run at a 1.91% median, plausibly because they are still accumulating engagement [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- **Comparability across accounts (depends on consistency, not estimator choice):** both methods can be applied identically to every profile; academic work shows ER itself is size-dependent — an inverted-U relationship between followers and engagement [Wies et al., JMR 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00222429221125131) — and ER-ranked influencers skew micro because "the engagement rate is generally inversely proportional to the number of followers" [ALCAM/ICWSM 2021](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/18060/17863/21555). Tier-stratified comparison is required under either estimator.
- **Validity as a business predictor (neither proven):** "Engagement rate is a screening metric. It isn't proof of authenticity or purchasing intent" [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide); Modash says "it shouldn't be the full verdict" and that a mega creator with 1% ER may deliver more total reach than a micro creator with 5% [Modash blog](https://www.modash.io/blog/influencer-engagement-rate); [Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it). An ICWSM study found influencers selected purely on high ER (7–12%) had only average audience retention and some had very high fraud scores [ALCAM/ICWSM 2021](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/18060/17863/21555).
- **Format confounding (both methods):** Reels, carousels and static posts engage differently and "a profile average hides that" [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator); a 245,559-post corpus found a median video post collected 117 engagements vs 41 for an image (2.85×), with the multiple ranging from 0.45× to 13.1× by category [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).

### Inferences
- Practical scorecard (relative judgement derived from the cited evidence, not a measured head-to-head test):

| Definition of "accuracy" | Median likes ÷ followers, ~3 months | Mean likes+comments ÷ followers, ≤12 posts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical/next-post performance | Stronger (outlier-resistant; stated design goal) | Weaker (one hit lifts the mean) |
| Stability over time | Stronger, but refresh lag and sparse-account risk | Weaker to moderate; fast-moving and sensitive to one post |
| Comparability across profiles | Equally consistent if applied uniformly; variable n by posting cadence | Equally consistent if applied uniformly; variable calendar span |
| Responsiveness to recent change | Slower (2–4 week refresh, 3-month pool) | Faster (rolling 12 posts) |
| Breadth of interaction captured | Narrower (likes only) | Slightly broader (comments ~1–2% of public interactions) |
| Link to business outcomes | Unproven | Unproven |

- The honest product framing is not "more accurate vs less accurate" but "**typical performance vs recent output**". If the product's job is show a current snapshot and rank on it, the 12-post mean is a coherent estimator; if the job is forecast a normal campaign post, the median is conceptually better.
- Adding comments to the numerator barely changes the number in public data, but it does change *comparability*: a likes-only ER cannot be placed on a likes+comments benchmark table even if the difference is small.
- Longer is not automatically better: if the underlying propensity is genuinely changing, a longer window trades responsiveness for stability, and academic work shows likes "dynamically change over time" [InfluencerRank, ICWSM 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/22162/21941/26225).

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study found that measures which of these two estimators has lower error against a true underlying engagement propensity or against future campaign performance.
- No source found quantifying the optimal post count/window for a follower-based public ER tradeoff between stability and responsiveness.
- No source found on how posting cadence (posts per month) changes the effective comparison window of a 12-post rule across accounts.

## Key Question 4: Are there known critiques of using Modash-style benchmarks from other practitioners? Are there critiques of short 12-post averages?

### Takeaway
Yes on both sides. Modash-style benchmarks are criticised for internally inconsistent "good" ranges, opaque sample definitions, mixing brands/creators and denominators, and tracked-data bias. Twelve-post averages are criticised for small-sample volatility, viral-post distortion, uneven time coverage, and undisclosed sample sizes. The strongest critiques come from competing analytics vendors, so commercial interest should be disclosed.

### Cited Findings
- **Critiques of Modash-style benchmarks and published tables:**
  - Modash's own help-page "good" ranges (3–7% for 10k–100k) contradict its 84M-profile medians (0.57% / 0.38%), and its other tier bands also sit above its own database medians [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
  - Benchmarks change under you: HypeAuditor changed its formula in 2026; Buffer notes that changes in dataset composition can move a median more than real platform performance does [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
  - Published headline numbers mix means and medians, brands and creators, followers and views/reach; "Socialinsider and Rival IQ publish brand-account data, and that says little about creators"; Socialinsider's page carries dating errors [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
  - Follower-tier boundaries are inconsistent across ten creator-ranking pages: "the definition of the micro tier alone start anywhere from 1,000 followers to 15,000 and end anywhere from 10,000 to 1,000,000" [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
  - Modash documentation is internally inconsistent on window (help page ~3 months vs calculator last 2 months) and on aggregation (Discovery median vs Campaign per-creator averages) [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools); [Modash campaign help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/13717084-understanding-campaign-analytics).
  - Likes-only ER omits comments, and no public-data ER can capture saves, shares, reach or DMs; hidden likes make it uncomputable [CreaSeed](https://creaseed.ai/compare/engagement-explain-modash); [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
  - Modash includes pinned posts in the ER window when many other tools exclude them — a reproducibility trap [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools).
  - "A properly labeled 0.6% tells you more than an unexplained 4% ever will" [Followerus](https://followerus.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate); "a chart saying 'a good Instagram engagement rate is 3–6%' ... [should state] what year, how many accounts, what follower sizes, brands or creators, what interactions, what denominator, mean or median, how many recent posts" [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide).
- **Critiques of short (12-post) averages:**
  - Small-sample problem: "A rate computed over two videos is not a measurement... Some read your last 12 posts, some the last 25 ... The number looks equally confident either way. Before you trust an engagement rate, ask how many posts it covers. Under ten, treat it as an indication. Over twenty, treat it as a measurement" [ProveitGo](https://www.proveitgo.com/blog/engagement-rate-different-every-tool).
  - Viral distortion: 21 of 38 popular accounts had an average at least twice their typical post [ToolzerHub](https://toolzerhub.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate); mean ER of 3.16% vs 1.0% median in a five-post illustration [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide); "averages describe outliers and medians describe you" [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks).
  - Same posted statistic from academic work: mean 0.038 vs median 0.029 across influencers [InfluencerRank, ICWSM 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/22162/21941/26225).
  - Practitioners recommend larger/median-based samples instead: 30 posts and median [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks); 20–30 posts, "be sceptical below about ten" [Xcut](https://xcut.ai/guides/how-to-find-outlier-posts); 9–12 posts averaged but "take the median if one viral post distorts the mean" [Forcletter](https://forcreator.co.kr/en/blog/instagram-engagement-rate); for the account owner, "Use the median of your last 12 Reels rather than any single post" [CreatorHouse](https://creatorhouse.app/blog/average-engagement-rate-on-instagram).
  - 12 posts can cover very different time spans; Virev imposed a 180-day cap and excluded posts younger than 3 days so likes had settled [Virev](https://virev.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks); OwlStat required activity in the prior 90 days [OwlStat](https://owlstat.io/instagram-engagement-benchmarks).
  - Sample-count bias in benchmark tables: CreatorHouse gave each account one number (median across its own Reels) so "the busiest account (269 Reels) counts exactly as much as one with five" [CreatorHouse](https://creatorhouse.app/blog/average-engagement-rate-on-instagram).

### Inferences
- The critiques are asymmetric in kind: Modash-style benchmarks are attacked mainly on **definition, transparency and internal consistency** (what does the table measure and where did the bands come from), while 12-post averages are attacked mainly on **sampling error and outliers** (how stable is this one number for this one account).
- A product can answer most of the Modash-style critique by publishing its own formula, sample, tier definitions, and date; it cannot fully answer the 12-post critique without either lengthening the window, adding a median, or showing a distribution/volatility signal.
- Source-quality caution: OWM, LikeWave, ProveitGo, SmartCreatorHub, CreaSeed and most benchmark pages are commercial content marketers with products in this space. Their *methodological* claims are mostly checkable against vendor docs and are used here for that reason; their "our tool is better" implications should not be imported into the final report.

### Gaps
- No independent, peer-reviewed critique specifically of Modash's benchmark table was found; the available critiques are practitioner/vendor publications.
- No peer-reviewed study specifically evaluating 12-post follower-based averages was found; the statistical critiques are general (outliers, sample size) applied to social data.
- No source found comparing Modash's benchmark bands against an independent ground truth of creator performance.

## Key Question 5: For a product that ranks creators with a fixed formula across all profiles, does changing to a median/3-month estimator improve or degrade ranking validity?

### Takeaway
There is no evidence that switching improves ranking validity. It changes what the ranking measures (typical post vs recent output), makes the product's numbers more consistent with one external provider (Modash) but less consistent with its own historical series and with other 12-post benchmark tables. Ranking validity depends more on applying one documented formula uniformly, stratifying by follower tier/format, and showing uncertainty than on mean vs median alone.

### Cited Findings
- Precedent for median-based ranking exists: Modash's help page says users can "sort results by engagement rate to surface the most engaged creators first" where Discovery ER is the median likes ÷ followers measure [Modash Help Center](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it); OWM filters and reports creator ER from Modash data [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks).
- But the same vendor uses a **different aggregation in its campaign dashboard** (per-creator averages), and explicitly says "the same creator may show different ER% values in each context ... Both are valid, they just measure slightly different things" — i.e. even a large vendor does not treat one estimator as universally correct [Modash campaign help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/13717084-understanding-campaign-analytics).
- Ranking "ground truth" is itself estimator-dependent: in an ICWSM influencer dataset the mean ER was 0.038 and the median 0.029 [InfluencerRank, ICWSM 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/22162/21941/26225); changing estimator changes who is on top even before data change.
- ER-based rankings have known construct problems: influencers ranked purely by 7–12% ER included accounts with average audience retention and high fraud scores [ALCAM/ICWSM 2021](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/18060/17863/21555); another academic ranking study notes likes "dynamically change over time" and treats temporal patterns as signal [InfluencerRank, ICWSM 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/22162/21941/26225).
- Academic work supports the size problem for any follower-based rank: engagement has an inverted-U relationship with follower count [Wies et al., JMR 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00222429221125131), and an influencer-marketing meta-analysis finds smaller influencers excel at engagement but that effectiveness is conditional on message type and context [JAMS meta-analysis 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-025-01107-3).
- A simple engagement baseline is a strong and simple ranker relative to follower count in academic influence-ranking work, but richer temporal/relational models outperform it — supporting "useful screen, not final verdict" [Measuring Influence on Instagram, arXiv 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1806.00881).
- Format mix is a hidden rank driver: median video posts collect ~2.85× the engagements of images, with category-level multiples from 0.45× to 13.1× [buzzabout](https://buzzabout.ai/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-benchmarks); "Reels, carousels, and static posts all engage differently. A profile average hides that" [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator).
- Practitioner framing for shortlists: "a comparable engagement metric helps you interpret a shortlist. It does not establish audience relevance, sentiment, authenticity, sales or a fair fee by itself" [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools); "A creator who has a modest public ER but a highly relevant audience may be a much better partner" [LikeWave](https://likewave.io/blog/instagram-engagement-rate-guide).

### Inferences
- **Verdict for the ranking use case:** switching a fixed-formula ranker from 12-post mean to median/~3 months would probably **improve stability and typical-post predictiveness**, but would **degrade responsiveness** and **break comparability with the product's own history and with 12-post peer benchmark tables**. Whether it improves *ranking validity* (correct ordering by campaign value) is unproven — no study found tests this head-to-head. For a fixed-formula product, "consistency across profiles" is already satisfied by either method; the consistency at risk is temporal and cross-provider.
- **Practical recommendation to the product owner:** do not present the change as an accuracy upgrade. Either (a) keep the 12-post mean as the headline, add a clearly-labelled "typical post" median and a volatility/outlier flag, and warn that one viral post can move the mean; or (b) if adopting a median, use a **fixed-count + bounded-window rule** (e.g. median of up to 30 posts from the last 90 days, with a minimum post count and explicit exclusions) rather than an unbounded "~3 months" that yields a different sample size per account. Publish the formula and recompute any benchmark tiers with it.
- **Disclosure language for ranking:** "Creators are ranked by [stated formula] applied identically to every public profile. This is a screening signal based on public likes and comments; it excludes saves, shares and reach, and it does not measure authenticity, audience fit or campaign results. Rankings can move when a post goes viral or when the measurement window rolls over. Compare creators within the same follower tier and content format."
- **Caveats that matter most, in order:** (1) formula mismatch with any external benchmark; (2) follower tier and brand-vs-creator population; (3) outlier/sample-size volatility; (4) format mix; (5) freshness and hidden-like exclusions.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found comparing rank correlation, top-k overlap, or business outcomes between a median/~3-month estimator and a 12-post mean on the same set of creators. This is the single biggest evidence gap for the product decision.
- No source found on user comprehension: whether product users interpret a median differently from an average, or whether rankings built on medians are perceived as more trustworthy.
- No source found on how often creator accounts have too few posts in a 3-month window for a stable median, or how Modash handles those cases.
