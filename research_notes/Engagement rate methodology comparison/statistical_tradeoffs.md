# Statistical validity of median-vs-mean and time-window-vs-post-count choices for Instagram engagement rate

Scope note: Estimator A = median likes per post ÷ followers, over roughly 3 months (exemplified by Modash's published formula). Estimator B = average (likes + comments) per post ÷ followers, over up to 12 recent posts. Both are follower-based, public-data estimators; the evidence below is organized around cross-account comparability and measurement of one account's current performance.

## Key Question 1: Under what conditions is a median more robust/valid than a mean for skewed engagement distributions (viral outliers, bot-like spikes, small samples)? What does the literature say about efficiency vs robustness, especially with 12 observations?

### Takeaway
The median is the more valid estimator of the *typical* post when engagement counts are heavy-tailed or contaminated by viral/bot-like spikes, because it has a 50% breakdown point and bounded influence whereas the mean has breakdown point 1/n and unbounded influence. The price is lower statistical efficiency under clean/normal data (asymptotic relative efficiency ≈ 2/π ≈ 64%) and small-sample quirks (upward mean-bias of the sample median under strong skew); at n≈12 neither estimator dominates in all senses, and a trimmed mean is a documented compromise.

### Cited Findings
- NIST's engineering statistics handbook: for data with extreme tails the median is generally a better estimate of location than the mean; the mean is optimal when data are normal. NIST distinguishes robustness of *validity* (confidence intervals maintain coverage under nonnormality) from robustness of *efficiency* (intervals stay nearly as narrow as the best possible); the median tends to have validity but not efficiency — [NIST](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm).
- Breakdown point of the mean is 1/n (a single arbitrarily large value can move it without limit); the median's breakdown point is about 50% and its influence function is bounded. The median also achieves the smallest max bias among location-equivariant estimators — [Rousseeuw 2011, WIREs](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/widm.2); [Rousseeuw 2019 slides](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~slovett/workshops/robust-statistics-2019/slides/donoho-univariate.pdf); [Statistics By Jim](https://statisticsbyjim.com/basics/robust-statistics).
- Asymptotic relative efficiency of the sample median vs the mean under normality is 2/π = 0.637 — [MATH3714 course notes](https://seehuhn.github.io/MATH3714/S19-efficiency.html). A simulation (n=100, 10,000 reps; secondary blog source) reports relative efficiency of median ≈0.654 under normal, ≈1.62 under t(3), ≈3.36 under normal contamination with 5% of points at 10× scale; a 10% trimmed mean was ≈0.948 under normal and ≈4.69 under contamination — [Risk Hub](https://riskhub.org/blogs/robust-measures-of-location-and-scale).
- A 20% trimmed mean is recommended in medical-education research as a compromise: nearly the power of the mean under normality, without the mean's outlier sensitivity; simply trimming and applying Student's t is described as technically unsound — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4205532).
- Small-sample caveat from Rousselet & Wilcox (2020): the sample median is mean-biased upward for small samples from skewed distributions (in their ex-Gaussian simulations, bias ≈15 ms for n=10 and ≈0.7 ms for n=100), while the sample mean is mean-unbiased. But the sample median is *median-unbiased* whereas the mean is *median-biased* under skew; bias can be corrected by percentile bootstrap; simulations of false/true positives showed "no method dominates," and the authors argue neither mean nor median alone is sufficient for comparing skewed distributions — [Meta-Psychology](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/1630); [full PDF](http://eprints.gla.ac.uk/189893/7/189893.pdf). A companion analysis reports the median's median-bias is already very small at n=10 and absent by n=15 in those simulations — [garstats](https://garstats.wordpress.com/2018/03/30/rtbias3).
- Instagram engagement counts are empirically heavy-tailed: log-log CCDFs of likes and comments are nearly straight lines, i.e., a limited number of posts receive most interactions; photos have a heavier tail than albums — [WebSci 2024, ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3614419.3644029); [PDF](https://www.ida.liu.se/~nikca89/papers/websci24.pdf). A power-law fit to Instagram likes is reported with exponent γ=1.391, while comments show two regimes and no significant single power law — [arXiv 1406.7751](https://arxiv.org/html/1406.7751v1). A 2025 modeling study fits Weibull/lognormal distributions and explicitly uses medians to summarize parameters because "some posts receive exceptionally high or low user reactions" — [Axioms 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9709/12/3/59).
- Concrete 12-post illustration (vendor demonstration, not peer-reviewed): for @nasa the mean of likes was 511,691 vs a median of 300,002 (factor 1.7), with best/worst post differing 34:1; five ways of computing a rate from the same 12-post pull ranged from 0.184% to 0.574% of followers — [monid.ai](https://monid.ai/blog/guides/are-instagram-engagement-trackers-accurate).
- Industry practice reflects the tradeoff: Modash's published Instagram formula is median likes ÷ followers, explicitly "so one viral post can't drag the whole figure up" — [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator); [Modash help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it). HubSpot recommends computing engagement over the last 10–20 posts and taking the median, "not the average" — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/instagram-engagement-rate). HypeAuditor likewise takes the median of per-content engagement rates — [HypeAuditor digest](https://hypeauditor.com/digest-august-26).
- Order-statistic subtlety: with 12 observations the median is the average of the 6th and 7th sorted values, so it uses only the central positions and discards the magnitudes of the extremes; the mean uses all 12 values including viral posts — [NIST](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm); [Statistics By Jim](https://statisticsbyjim.com/basics/robust-statistics). Also, a median of per-post ratios is not generally a ratio of medians or a pooled interaction-to-exposure rate; an illustrative example gives 15% vs 10% vs ≈4.62% for the same three posts — [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools).

### Inferences
- Estimator A's median is defending against exactly the pathologies documented in the engagement literature: a few posts capturing most interactions, and bot/engagement-pod spikes (bot comments are detected at scale in influencer data — [Kim & Han 2020](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-60975-7_10)). For predicting a *typical* next post or ranking accounts on consistent performance, A is statistically better motivated than B.
- Estimator B's mean is not "wrong": it is nearer to an unbiased estimate of the *average* engagement produced per post (including the viral upside), uses all observations, and is more efficient if a clean, roughly symmetric regime holds. For campaign planning around total engagement delivered across a set of posts, a mean-type quantity can be the right target. The word "valid" depends on the estimand.
- At n=12 the robustness gain is real but the precision cost is nontrivial: the median is determined by two central observations, its sampling distribution is skewed for strongly skewed data, and the efficiency loss versus the mean is expected to be sizable under normality (asymptotically ~36%). The Rousselet & Wilcox result implies small-sample median bias could systematically favor accounts with certain skew shapes when n differs across accounts — an argument for equal n (see Q2) or bootstrap bias correction.
- A 10–20% trimmed or winsorized mean would capture most of the robustness with less efficiency loss than the median ([Rousseeuw slides](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~slovett/workshops/robust-statistics-2019/slides/donoho-univariate.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4205532)); no source found applies this specifically to Instagram engagement rates.
- Because A uses likes only and B uses likes+comments (see Q3), and because median-of-ratios ≠ ratio-of-medians, an observed A-vs-B difference cannot be attributed to the mean/median choice alone; the numerator and aggregation must be held constant to isolate it.

### Gaps
- No source found reporting the *exact* finite-sample relative efficiency of the median vs mean at n=12 for a lognormal/Pareto-like engagement distribution; only the asymptotic normal figure (0.637) and a simulation at n=100 were located.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that quantifies the sampling variance or confidence interval of an Instagram account-level median-likes rate based on 12 posts vs 90 days.
- No study found specifically evaluating whether bot-like engagement spikes are better handled by median vs trimmed mean in influencer metrics.
- The impact of hidden like counts (which make A unavailable) on comparability is noted by Modash but not quantified — [Modash help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it).

## Key Question 2: How does posting cadence bias a fixed 3-month window versus a fixed 12-post window? Concrete examples and recency vs stability.

### Takeaway
Neither window is cadence-neutral: a 3-month window yields variable n (and therefore variable precision and outlier sensitivity), while a 12-post window yields equal n but variable calendar coverage (and therefore variable recency and exposure to pinned/stale posts). A 3-month window is also aligned with Instagram's own 90-day Insights limit and favors stability; 12 posts favors current performance and equalized precision.

### Cited Findings
- Instagram brand posting cadence varies widely: Vista Social reports Instagram median 4.2 posts/week, 25th percentile 1.7, 75th percentile 9.4, top decile 19.5 (15,674 profiles, Aug–Oct 2026) — [Vista Social](https://vistasocial.com/research/social-media-benchmarks). Buffer reports an average of 19 Instagram posts per month across its benchmark sample — [Buffer](https://buffer.com/social-media-benchmarks/instagram); Socialinsider reports ~20 posts/month for brands — [ClickMinded compilation](https://www.clickminded.com/social-media-benchmarks/).
- Instagram Insights allows preset/custom date ranges only within the past 90 days — [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/1533933820244654); [About Instagram insights](https://help.instagram.com/788388387972460).
- Interactions are front-loaded and short-lived: median time for a post to accumulate 95% of its interactions is 33 hours on Instagram (mean 55 hours); about 50% of interactions arrive within the first 2 hours on Instagram — [Springer, SN Applied Network Science 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13278-022-00928-2); [ASONAM PDF](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/e384c434-2f87-d4b2-e053-9f05fe0a1d67/ASONAM_VASSIO_Accepted.pdf). New posts progressively fade older posts' attractiveness — same sources.
- Posting frequency is itself related to per-post engagement, not just to sample size: WebSci 2024 found users with fewer than 20 posts in the prior week received more interactions per post than very high-frequency uploaders, with likes and comments behaving somewhat differently — [ACM WebSci 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3614419.3644029). By contrast, Vista Social found Instagram median engagement rate by impressions nearly flat across posting-frequency bands (2.8%, 2.7%, 2.7%, 3.0% for <1, 1–3, 3–7, ≥7 posts/week) — [Vista Social](https://vistasocial.com/research/social-media-benchmarks). These two findings should be treated as conflicting/context-dependent, not reconciled.
- Pinned posts can distort a "recent 12" sample: in one 12-post audit, three pinned posts kept accumulating likes; dropping them changed the mean from 0.490% to 0.437% and the median from 0.287% to 0.248% of followers — [monid.ai](https://monid.ai/blog/guides/are-instagram-engagement-trackers-accurate) (vendor demonstration). A commercial methodology page likewise excludes pinned posts and very fresh posts from its engagement calculation — [lookfluence methodology](https://lookfluence.com/methodology).
- Tool conventions differ on window type: Modash help describes "roughly the last three months" of content [Modash help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it), while its public calculator page describes "the last two months" [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator) — a documented internal inconsistency. HypeAuditor uses the latest 30 publications — [HypeAuditor help](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it). A 12-post baseline is described as "a practical window rather than a magic number" — [SirenCY](https://www.sirency.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate).

### Inferences
- Arithmetic illustration (my calculation from the cited cadence distribution, not a cited empirical result): at 1 post/week a 3-month window contains ≈13 posts, so 12 posts ≈12 weeks of history; at 4 posts/week it contains ≈52 posts, so 12 posts ≈3 weeks; at the 75th-percentile cadence of 9.4 posts/week, 12 posts ≈9 days. Thus Estimator A samples ~4–10× more posts for a fast account and B samples a ~3-month period for a slow account and a ~1–3-week period for a fast one.
- Cross-account validity: B equalizes n, which equalizes the influence of a single outlier and gives roughly comparable precision (the median/mean variance both fall with n; see Q1). A leaves n uncontrolled, so a slow account's rate is noisier and more outlier-driven than a fast account's — a systematic bias in rankings if cadence correlates with account type.
- Cross-account validity: A equalizes *calendar exposure*, so all accounts are measured over the same recent season/algorithm regime; B equalizes *post count* but not time, so a fast account's number describes the last few weeks while a slow account's describes a quarter. Given rapid interaction decay and algorithm/content-mix changes, B's "current performance" claim is stronger for fast accounts and weaker for slow ones.
- Recency vs stability: for an individual account's current state, B's recency is an advantage if the account recently changed format or quality; A's longer window is an advantage if the goal is a stable baseline less sensitive to a single good/bad week. The 90-day Insights ceiling makes ~3 months the practical maximum for first-party data.
- Cadence is not merely a nuisance parameter: because higher posting can reduce interactions per post in one large academic dataset and leave per-impression engagement flat in another, a post-count window can partially control exposure to the "many posts compete for attention" effect (fewer posts included for fast accounts) but does not remove cadence as a confounder in cross-account comparisons.
- A hybrid (e.g., last 90 days with a minimum post count, or last 12 posts plus a reported date span and dispersion) would address both failure modes; such a design is not evaluated in the sources found.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head empirical study found comparing the test–retest reliability or cross-account ranking stability of a 3-month window vs a 12-post window.
- No source found specifying a principled minimum number of posts for a stable account-level median/mean Instagram rate (industry guidance of 10–20 posts or 30 publications is heuristic — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/instagram-engagement-rate); [HypeAuditor help](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it)).
- Unknown how often pinned/hidden-like posts occur by account type, so the size of that bias in practice is unquantified.
- No source found quantifying seasonal/algorithm drift within 90 days vs 12 posts for the same account.

## Key Question 3: Does including comments (and excluding them) materially change what the metric measures? What do standard industry definitions say engagement rate includes?

### Takeaway
Standard industry and platform definitions treat engagement as a bundle of actions (likes, comments, saves, shares, and sometimes more); likes-only and likes+comments are both recognizable but partial conventions. Comments are a distinct, rarer, more effortful signal, so including them changes the construct more than it usually changes the magnitude — though for some accounts comments are a large share of interactions.

### Cited Findings
- Instagram's own documentation defines Interactions as "the number of actions people take when they engage with your content, such as likes, comments, saves and shares"; post-level data include likes, comments, shares, saves; reach and "reach from non-followers" are separate, estimated metrics — [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/788388387972460); [post insights](https://help.instagram.com/553860108359435); [Commerce Manager CSV definitions](https://help.instagram.com/825941707897287).
- Hootsuite lists comments, shares, likes, saves, mentions, branded hashtags, click-throughs, DMs and accounts engaged as possible engagement metrics, and gives several formulas (by post, reach, impressions, daily) — [Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/instagram-engagement); [Hootsuite 2026 update](https://blog.hootsuite.com/instagram-engagement-rate). Hootsuite notes that a like vs a send/comment reflect different levels of effort — [Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/instagram-engagement).
- Sprout Social defines Instagram engagement as likes, comments, saves, shares and other actions, and offers by-follower, by-reach and by-impression denominators — [Sprout Social](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/instagram-engagement-rate). HubSpot's formula is (likes + comments + saves + shares) ÷ followers × 100 — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/instagram-engagement-rate).
- Academic operationalizations: a Journal of Marketing Research study treats likes, comments and mentions as reflective indicators of post engagement measured up to two days after posting — [Wies, Bleier & Edeling 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222429221125131). A systematic review of social-media-engagement measurement finds heterogeneous "normalized indexes" divided by posts, followers or reached people — [Italian Journal of Marketing review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43039-021-00035-8).
- Public-data conventions diverge: Socialinsider defines engagement rate per post as (likes + comments) ÷ followers — [Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram); Modash's published Instagram rate uses median likes ÷ followers — [Modash](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator). Saves and shares are private to outsiders, which is why HypeAuditor excludes them — [HypeAuditor help](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it); [lookfluence methodology](https://lookfluence.com/methodology).
- Likes and comments are empirically distinct, not interchangeable: Spearman ρ=0.58 (p<0.001) between their distributions; comments are less frequent, costlier and expose opinions more, while likes are immediate — [arXiv 2301.06815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06815); [ACM version](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3578503.3583623). A thesis explicitly separates "active" (comments) from "passive" (likes) engagement rates — [Boftsi 2020](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=150903).
- Magnitudes vary by account: a study of Spanish health influencers reports 3.7% likes per publication vs 0.91% comments for one account — [MDPI Social Sciences 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0760/12/4/220). A vendor sample of 38 accounts found comments were a median 1.6% of likes+comments, but one account's comments made up 39.4% — [ToolzerHub](https://toolzerhub.com/blog/instagram-engagement-rate) (vendor source, treat cautiously).
- Engagement bots and pods specifically manufacture likes and short, similar comments, so comment inclusion can add fabricated as well as authentic signal — [Kim & Han 2020](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-60975-7_10); [fake-follower descriptive study](https://ijcrt.org/papers/IJCRT2012326.pdf).

### Inferences
- For cross-account comparison using public data, likes+comments is closer to the industry/platform notion of "interactions," so Estimator B's numerator has better *content validity* than A's likes-only numerator — even though B's mean has weaker robustness properties. A better measures "typical light-touch response per follower"; B better measures "typical total publicly visible interaction per follower."
- In most accounts comments are a small fraction of likes, so adding them changes the number modestly (consistent with the 1.6% median in the vendor sample) but can change rankings when a comment-heavy account is in the set (39.4% case). The effect is therefore account-mix dependent, not a constant offset.
- Because likes and comments correlate only moderately and have different tails, summing them can partly diversify away single-action noise, but it also merges two constructs; the academic recommendation to analyze them separately—or at least report them separately—is well supported.
- Saves/shares would broaden the construct further but are inaccessible to third-party comparisons; this means A and B cannot be made equivalent to first-party "engagement rate" definitions without account access.
- Any comparison of A and B must state the numerator convention; otherwise differences in central-tendency choice and in numerator scope are confounded.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that quantifies how much adding comments changes cross-account rank order of engagement rate for a large Instagram sample.
- No source found comparing the heavy-tailedness (tail index) of comments vs likes at the *account* level over 12 posts; the arXiv result is across the platform and the WebSci result across post types.
- Unknown whether comment counts are more or less affected than likes by hidden/unavailable counts in current Instagram data.

## Key Question 4: What are the known problems with follower count as the denominator (follower growth, fake followers, reach vs followers)?

### Takeaway
Both estimators inherit the same denominator weakness: follower count is a changing, partially fake, and imperfect proxy for who actually saw a post. Neither the median nor the mean fixes this; it is a construct-validity issue that is at least as consequential as the numerator/aggregation choice.

### Cited Findings
- Instagram reports follower growth (gains/losses) as a trend metric; standard benchmarks report average Instagram follower growth around 7.5% per month — [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/788388387972460); [Buffer](https://buffer.com/social-media-benchmarks/instagram). A fraud benchmark reports an average daily change in influencer follower count of 1.2%, treating much larger shifts as a red flag — [Captiv8 2018 report](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/4274052/%23NoFilter-How%20to%20Uncover%20the%20Fakes_Captiv8%20Report_Final.pdf). Longitudinal academic data show influencer follower counts grow over time with highly variable, influencer-specific rates — [Springer 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13278-022-00928-2).
- Fake/inactive followers dilute follower-based rates: a Captiv8 analysis of 5,000 randomly selected influencer accounts per category over a 90-day 2018 period found >11% of engagement on sponsored Instagram posts came from fraudulent accounts — [Captiv8 PDF](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/4274052/%23NoFilter-How%20to%20Uncover%20the%20Fakes_Captiv8%20Report_Final.pdf). Bot-like engagement is detectable at scale (14,221 influencers, 65.8M engagements; short, similar comments; model ≈80% accuracy) — [Kim & Han 2020](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-60975-7_10). Contract-theoretic work predicts most faking at intermediate follower counts — [IIMB working paper 2022](https://www.iimb.ac.in/sites/default/files/2022-03/WP%20No.%20580%20%282022%29.pdf). Experiments document a "mere number effect" whereby large follower counts still raise perceived influence even when fake followers are signaled — [Journal of Business Research 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296322010542).
- Reach is a different quantity: Instagram defines reach as unique accounts seeing content, includes "reach from non-followers," and labels reach estimated — [Instagram Commerce Manager definitions](https://help.instagram.com/825941707897287). Some Instagram viewer/viewer figures are explicitly "estimated and in-development" — [About Instagram insights](https://help.instagram.com/788388387972460).
- HypeAuditor changed its Instagram ER to (likes + comments) ÷ views when enough Reels exist, with a follower-based fallback, stating that "follower count is no longer a reliable proxy for how many people see a piece of content" — [HypeAuditor digest](https://hypeauditor.com/digest-august-26); [HypeAuditor help](https://help.hypeauditor.com/en/articles/2771955-what-is-engagement-rate-and-how-do-you-calculate-it). Hootsuite calls engagement rate by reach the most common formula and recommends reach/impression-based formulas for content effectiveness — [Hootsuite 2026](https://blog.hootsuite.com/instagram-engagement-rate); Sprout offers all three denominators — [Sprout Social](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/instagram-engagement-rate).
- The choice of denominator changes benchmark levels by an order of magnitude: Modash's median likes÷followers runs from 3.51% (1–5k followers) to 0.38–0.57% (10k–100k), whereas HypeAuditor's 2026 US likes+comments÷views medians are ~3.72–4.33% across tiers — [OWM compilation of both sources](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks), [Modash calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator).
- Measurement-standard bodies require disclosure of data-collection method, computation of estimates, reliability/sampling error, limitations and unusual conditions in reported periods — [MRC Social Media Measurement Guidelines v1.0](https://mediaratingcouncil.org/sites/default/files/Standards/MRC%20Social%20Measurement%20Guidelines%20v1.0%20Final.pdf) (search-result excerpt; direct PDF fetch was unsupported by the fetch tool, so exact wording should be verified by the report writer).

### Inferences
- With ~7.5%/month average follower growth, a 90-day window can see ~10–25% denominator growth; applying a single current follower count to all posts in the window systematically understates earlier posts' rates. A 12-post window is shorter for fast accounts (less drift) but longer for slow accounts (more drift), so the denominator problem interacts with cadence exactly as in Q2.
- Fake followers make both A and B downward-biased relative to the "real audience" rate, while bought comments/engagement pods push B upward; the direction of net bias is unknown for a given account.
- Follower-based rates are not scale-invariant: published medians fall steeply with account size, so cross-account comparison requires tier stratification regardless of median-vs-mean or window choice.
- Reach/views-based rates answer a different question (response among people actually exposed) and are less distorted by fake followers or growth, but are unavailable for third-party accounts and may themselves be estimated; thus A and B are appropriate mainly for public-account screening, not as definitive performance measures.
- Methodological disclosure (numerator, denominator, window, aggregation, extraction date) is essential; without it, A and B numbers are not comparable even within the same provider.

### Gaps
- No source found quantifying the within-account bias in an engagement rate caused specifically by using end-of-window follower count for all posts.
- No causal estimate found of how much fake followers change a typical account's rate; the >11% figure is a 2018 industry benchmark for sponsored posts, not a current platform-wide estimate.
- Unknown how current Instagram hidden-like and private-metric changes affect denominator-based comparability.

## Key Question 5: Are there empirical studies of influencer engagement rate measurement, benchmark comparability, or reliability over time?

### Takeaway
There is substantial literature on heavy-tailed engagement, influencer-tier effects and benchmark-definition heterogeneity, plus a few studies that use follower-based engagement rates as variables; however, I found no direct psychometric/test–retest study of the account-level engagement-rate estimators themselves. Benchmarks disagree largely because formulas, windows, account populations and aggregation methods differ.

### Cited Findings
- A Journal of Advertising Research study of 180 influencers and >35,000 posts across five platforms states that methods for evaluating influencers and choosing partners are "unreliable," and reports platform/tier-dependent "double jeopardy" effects for followers and engagement rate — [JAR 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2501/JAR-2023-008).
- "Follow Us and Become Famous!" finds influencer tier and category significantly affect likes/followers and comments/followers, and argues likes and comments must be analyzed separately (ρ=0.58) — [arXiv 2301.06815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06815); [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3578503.3583623).
- WebSci 2024 (1,000 users, 448,697 posts) documents heavy-tailed interactions, post-type differences and posting-frequency effects, using nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis, Mann–Whitney, bootstrap) rather than relying on means alone — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3614419.3644029); [PDF](https://www.ida.liu.se/~nikca89/papers/websci24.pdf).
- JMR 2023 uses prior engagement rate (average likes+comments per post over past 90 days ÷ followers) as a control and measures post engagement within two days of posting — [Wies et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00222429221125131). A 2026 study operationalizes follower engagement as average monthly likes/comments per post and treats posting consistency (volume and interval) as a separate predictor — [Taylor & Francis 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13527266.2026.2647893).
- Benchmark comparability: the same account can yield ~0.5% or ~4% depending on denominator (followers vs views), and published "good ER" guidance can contradict the same provider's own database medians — [OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks). A worked example shows median-of-ratios, ratio-of-medians and pooled rate giving 15%, 10% and ≈4.62% for the same three posts — [SmartCreatorHub](https://www.smartcreatorhub.blog/en/instagram-engagement-rate-different-tools). A 12-post audit produced five rates from 0.184% to 0.574% for one account — [monid.ai](https://monid.ai/blog/guides/are-instagram-engagement-trackers-accurate).
- Reliability evidence that does exist concerns *self-report* engagement scales, not behavioral ER: an adolescent social-media engagement scale showed 8-week test–retest reliability above 0.68 — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00701/full); a systematic review notes the scarcity of validated engagement measures and platform-specific measurement problems — [Computers in Human Behavior review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563218300293); [Trifiro & Gerson 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2056305119848743).
- Data-collection reliability more generally is not automatic: a study of Twitter API collection found correlations of 0.89–0.92 across repeated Search API runs but only ~0.42–0.43 between Search and Streaming API datasets — [AAAI-affiliated PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/14669/14669-28-18187-1-2-20201228.pdf).
- MRC-style standards call for disclosing collection, computation, reliability/limitations — [MRC guidelines](https://mediaratingcouncil.org/sites/default/files/Standards/MRC%20Social%20Measurement%20Guidelines%20v1.0%20Final.pdf) (verify exact text; PDF fetch failed).

### Inferences
- The empirical literature supports the qualitative claims behind both A and B (skew/outliers matter; tier and category matter; cadence and post type matter) but does not settle which of these two specific estimator recipes is more valid.
- Benchmark comparisons are only meaningful within the same formula, window, aggregation method and account population; A's benchmark family (Modash median likes/followers) and B's (likes+comments/followers averages, e.g., Socialinsider/Rival IQ-style) are not interchangeable — [Modash](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator); [Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram); [ClickMinded](https://www.clickminded.com/social-media-benchmarks/).
- Reliability of an account-level rate over time is plausibly affected by three separable components: sampling noise from few posts, real temporal drift and denominator drift; no source found decomposes these.
- Source-quality caveat for the report writer: several 2026 vendor/blog pages (monid.ai, ToolzerHub, SirenCY, SmartCreatorHub, OWM, LikeWave) provide useful transparent worked examples and methodology comparisons but are commercial/secondary sources, not independent peer-reviewed validation; treat their numbers as illustrations. Documented conflicts include Modash's two-month vs three-month descriptions ([Modash help](https://help.modash.io/en/articles/6542471-understanding-engagement-rate-and-how-modash-calculates-it) vs [calculator](https://www.modash.io/engagement-rate-calculator)) and WebSci's vs Vista Social's differing conclusions on posting frequency.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that directly estimates test–retest reliability (ICC, correlation, or rank stability) of Instagram account engagement rate measured from 12 posts vs 90 days.
- No study found that validates either estimator against a ground-truth outcome (e.g., conversions, true reach, or sustained audience response) for the same accounts.
- No independent replication found of the vendor demonstrations quantifying mean/median and window effects (e.g., 1.7× likes ratio, 0.184–0.574% rate range).
- Unknown whether benchmark medians are stable when provider samples/compositions change; one secondary source says dataset composition can move medians more than real performance ([OWM](https://www.owm.ai/learn/creator-sourcing/engagement-rate-benchmarks)), but no quantification was found.
